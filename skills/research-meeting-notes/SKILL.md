# Research meeting notes

## Establish the record

Read or listen through all supplied material. Identify separate meetings, repeated exports, overlapping segments, and handoffs between participants’ projects. Use distinctive passages to establish overlap and sequence. Keep alternative readings where versions differ. Record the source type, available coverage, meeting date and its basis. If the date is unknown, say so; a file modification or preparation date describes the file. Name the participants identified by the user or source and state how individual turns were attributed.

Choose the level of detail the user requested. For edited transcripts, keep all substantive exchanges in source order. For verbatim or interaction analysis, retain repetitions, false starts, overlaps and pauses as supported by the recording. For working notes alone, capture the questions addressed, claims, evidence, disagreements, decisions and tasks. For live note-taking, give each topic a short cue, record source times or turns during the discussion when feasible, and write a short synthesis after the topic closes. If a recording or text is missing, work from the remaining material and describe the gap.

## Transcribe

1. Preserve the wording when the input is a text transcript. For a readable edition, adjust punctuation, paragraph breaks and unambiguous spellings. Drop empty fillers only if consistent with the declared style. Retain negatives, hedges, corrections, technical distinctions, numbers and dissent. Attribute an unverified experimental result to the speaker who reported it. Use quotation marks in the summary only for wording checked against the source.
2. Give each turn a stable speaker label where supported. Preserve supplied speaker labels or use a role, “Unidentified speaker,” or “speaker uncertain.” For a block of text with unclear turn changes, mark the exchange as unattributed. A name on the attendance list alone cannot identify a specific turn. Retain a brief interruption if it changes the interpretation or identifies the next speaker.
3. For audio, note actual time offsets at topic boundaries and ambiguous passages. Replay unclear words before marking them. Use “[inaudible]” or “[overlapping speech]” for audible limitations and “[unclear in supplied text]” or “[text ends here]” for text limitations. If only notes or fragments exist, label any reconstruction as a paraphrase. Give readers the available original wording for a consequential uncertain term.
4. Use an existing time, line, paragraph or turn reference for each section. For unnumbered material, assign stable IDs in source order and attach them to the passages. Use separate identifiers for different sources if needed. Where convenient, make summary references clickable.

## Make the summary usable

Start with the outcome in one or two sentences. Split separate projects into clearly named sections even if speakers suggested the same technique for both. Adjust the length of each section to the material and the user’s purpose.

- **Highlights.** Give the concrete claim, observation, result or advice that changed the discussion. Identify who reported it and their caveats where relevant.
- **Themes.** Express the recurring question or tradeoff in language specific to the meeting. Explain why it matters and link it to the turns that illustrate it.
- **Decisions and open questions.** Record what was agreed, rejected or left unresolved. An idea followed by a topic change remains a proposal.
- **Next steps.** Reconstruct a task from the full exchange. Record the action, its status, speaker or owner, and timing if given. Use “unassigned” or “no deadline stated” where a field matters and is missing. Preserve relative deadlines when the meeting date is unknown.
- **Call to action.** Surface the highest-priority explicit request for the intended reader. If no one set a priority, any concrete suggested first move should be labeled as the note-taker’s recommendation. When no action is called for, say so.

Trace each decision, action and material highlight back to its supporting passage. Merge references where a short paragraph relies on one exchange. Omit empty optional sections unless the user requested them.

## Review and deliver

Compare the transcript with each supplied source, including the first and last passage, topic handoffs and duplicates. Check numbers, dates, speaker attributions, technical terms and action ownership in a second pass. Review each substantive summary sentence at its anchor, including any qualification in the next turn. Report unreconciled conflicts, missing material, and the kind of verification actually performed.

Preserve source originals when saving an edited copy. Apply the requested audience and access scope to the deliverable. When a public edition is requested, check any stated restrictions and remove unrelated identifiers, private asides and confidential material when appropriate. Mark substantive redactions in the public edition and keep them out of public file history. Keep a fuller restricted edition when useful and permitted. Consult [Practice and evidence](references/practice-and-evidence.md) when the user asks for the rationale, an unfamiliar use requires choosing a transcription convention, or an audience change affects release.

**Worked example.** In T12 an adviser asks “could we try five examples?” In T13 a researcher responds “I’ll review two on Friday.” Record a five-example trial as proposed and a two-example review as the researcher’s commitment for Friday. The number, owner and date belong to the second task.
